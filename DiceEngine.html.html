Lesson 01 · Start here

You are about to write a program that rolls dice. Before you can write it, you have to know what a die actually does — and what a computer does when it pretends to be one.

Roll it five times and the bars look like nothing. Roll it five hundred times and they flatten out toward that dashed line. The line is theoretical probability — one face out of six, 1/6, 16.7% — and you get it by counting the faces, not by rolling. The bars are experimental probability: what actually happened.

They pull together as the count climbs. That is the law of large numbers, and it is worth being precise about what it does not promise. A face is never "due." The die has no memory. Each roll is an independent event, and a run of four 1s tells you exactly nothing about the fifth roll. Believing otherwise is the gambler's fallacy, and it is how casinos pay for the carpet.

Lesson 02 · Probability

Two dice are not one die twice as big.

Here is the mistake almost everybody makes: "two dice total 2 through 12, that's eleven results, so each is 1 in 11." Wrong — and the chart below shows you why in about four seconds.

The two dice are separate objects. Die A lands six ways; for each of those, die B lands six ways. That is 6 × 6 = 36 equally likely outcomes, and those 36 are not spread evenly across the totals. One pair makes a 2 (1 and 1). One pair makes a 12 (6 and 6). But six different pairs make a 7 — 1•6, 2•5, 3•4, 4•3, 5•2, 6•1. A seven is six times more likely than a twelve.

Push the dice slider to 5 or 6 and watch the shape. It stops being a triangle and becomes a bell. More dice means the extremes need more things to go right at once, so the middle swallows everything. This is why 3d6 feels completely different from 1d18 even though both run 3 to 18: the d18 is flat, and 3d6 clusters hard around 10 and 11. When you tune a game's damage or loot, this curve is the dial you are turning.

Design check. Your roller is going to take a number of dice and a number of sides. Every combination a player types produces one of these curves. Knowing the shape is the difference between balancing a game and guessing at it.
Lesson 03 · Where randomness lives

A rolling die is not actually random.

Physics says the face was decided the moment the die left your hand. That is not a technicality. It is the whole reason a computer can imitate one.

A die in the air is a solid object obeying Newton's laws. Position, spin, velocity, the springiness of the table, the air in the room — feed all of it into the math and the landing face falls out. People have built machines that do this. The die is unpredictable to you because the answer depends so violently on the starting conditions that a thousandth of a degree of tilt flips it. That property is called sensitive dependence on initial conditions, better known as chaos. Weather, shuffled cards, and coin flips are all in this family: deterministic, but hopeless to predict. The information exists. You just can't get at it.

Then there is the other kind, and it is different in nature rather than in difficulty. Take one atom of a radioactive isotope. It will decay at some point. Physics gives you the probability that it decays in the next second, but nothing inside that atom — no timer, no hidden variable anyone has ever found — decides when. Two identical atoms, prepared identically, decay at different times. As far as modern physics can tell, the answer does not exist anywhere in advance. That is true randomness.

When engineers need the real thing, they go outside the computer and measure the physical world. A Geiger counter watching a radioactive sample. Thermal noise — the electrons in any warm resistor jitter, and Intel ships that as a processor instruction called RDRAND. The site random.org listens to atmospheric radio static, mostly distant lightning. Cloudflare, which carries a large slice of the world's web traffic, points a camera at roughly a hundred lava lamps in its San Francisco lobby; London uses a double pendulum, Lisbon a bank of wave machines. It reads like a joke. It is load-bearing infrastructure.

Notice what every one of those has in common. The computer had to reach outside itself. It cannot make randomness internally. Next section is why.

Lesson 04 · Inside the machine

So it fakes it. Here is the actual formula.

A computer is engineered to be the opposite of random: same input, same output, every time. That is the point — you would not trust a calculator that changed its mind on Tuesdays. So when you ask for a random number, it runs arithmetic instead.

The trick is a pseudorandom number generator, or PRNG. Pseudo means fake. You hand it a starting number called the seed; it does some math; the answer is both your "random" number and the seed for the next one. The classic recipe is the linear congruential generator:

next_seed = (a * seed + c) mod m        // multiply, add, chop off the top
face      = (next_seed mod sides) + 1

Java's own java.util.Random is exactly this machine, and Oracle publishes the
    constants. It keeps a 48-bit seed and grinds it with
    seed = (seed × 0x5DEECE66D + 0xB) mod 248. The
    period — how many numbers it produces before the whole sequence restarts — is
    248, about 281 trillion. Huge, but finite, and finite is the point: a formula with a fixed amount of
    memory must eventually land on a state it has been in before, and from that instant it is doomed to repeat.

    
Where does the first seed come from? Usually the clock. new Random()
    with empty parentheses grabs the current time in nanoseconds, so every run feels different. Hand it a seed
    yourself and it stops being surprising at all.

What about AI?

    
A language model has no random bone in it either. Give it a prompt and it computes a probability for every
    possible next word, using fixed arithmetic on fixed numbers. Something still has to choose one. That
    chooser is a sampler, and the sampler pulls from a PRNG — the same clock-seeded arithmetic your dice program
    is about to use. A setting called temperature stretches or flattens those probabilities to make
    the pick more adventurous or more predictable, but it adds no randomness; it only reshapes the distribution being
    sampled. Fix the seed, set temperature to zero, and the model writes the same sentence word for word, every time.
    The creativity is a large pile of arithmetic drawing lots from a small pile of arithmetic.

    
When the stakes are real — encryption keys, login tokens, shuffling a deck for money — pseudorandom is
    not good enough, because whoever learns your seed owns every number you will ever produce. That is when a program
    reaches for SecureRandom, which asks the operating system for entropy scraped off the
    physical world: keystroke timing, disk interrupts, electrical noise, RDRAND. The computer is still faking it.
    It is just refilling the fake from something real.

  







  
Lesson 05 · The bug nobody sees

  
Six does not divide evenly into anything a computer likes.

  
Your generator hands you a number from a big range. You need a number from 1 to 6. Folding one into

Drag the states slider to 16 and the faces slider to 6. Sixteen states, six faces: 16 ÷ 6 is 2 remainder
    4, so four of the faces get three states and two of them get two. Faces 1 through 4 come up 18.75% of the time and
    faces 5 and 6 come up 12.5%. No choice of multiplier or increment repairs this, because the problem is not the
    generator. It is that 6 does not divide 16.

    
Press Apply Java's fix and the leftover states go grey. That is exactly what
    nextInt(bound) does: it spots the values that would land in the uneven tail, throws
    them away, and draws again. Costs a rare extra draw; buys a genuinely fair die. This is one of the reasons to call
    rng.nextInt(6) instead of writing your own mod 6, and it is
    a good answer to give when somebody asks why you didn't "just do the math yourself."

  


  
The other trap, and it is the one you will actually hit.
  nextInt(6) returns 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, or 5 — it starts at zero and stops one
  short of six. Forget the + 1 and your die grows a blank face and loses its six. Add one
  to the bound instead of the result and it grows a seventh. Both compile. Both run. Neither is a die.

Lesson 07 · Your turn

  
Now break it, bend it, and prove you understand it.

  
Pick at least two. The first one is the one I most want to see working.


  

    

      Challenge 01
      
Loaded dice

      
Make a 6 come up twice as often as any other face, using nothing but nextInt.
      Hint: ask for a range bigger than six, then decide what to do with the extra value. Prove it worked by rolling
      60,000 times and printing the tally.

    

    

      Challenge 02
      
Advantage and disadvantage

      
Roll 2d20 and keep the higher one. Then a version that keeps the lower. Roll each 10,000 times and print the
      average. Before you run it, write down what you think the two averages will be. Then find out how wrong you were.

    

    

      Challenge 03
      
Seeded replay

      
Add a menu option that lets the player type a seed. Same seed, same rolls, every time. Now you can reproduce a
      bug report, or hand a friend a number and have them play your exact game.

    

    

      Challenge 04
      
Histogram in the console

      
After N rolls, print a bar chart out of asterisks — one row per possible total, one star per 1% of the
      rolls. Run 5d6 and watch lesson 2's bell curve appear in your own terminal.

    

    

      Challenge 05
      
The exploding die

      
Roll a d6. If it comes up 6, roll again and add it. Keep going while you keep rolling sixes. What is the
      highest total you can get? What is the average? Why is this loop a genuine risk of never ending, and why is that
      risk effectively zero?

    

    

      Challenge 06
      
Prove the bias

      
Write a generator using the tiny LCG from lesson 4 — (5 × seed + 3) mod 16
      — and roll a d6 with it 60,000 times. Print the tally. Explain the shape of the result in one sentence.
